## Framework selection menu

This menu combines learning theories, instructional design approaches, evidence-informed practices, and accessibility guidance. They are different kinds of resources, with different evidence and purposes. The prompts are proposed authoring choices, not claims that every method works equally well. Retrieve a framework's primary source before making a precise historical, causal, or effectiveness claim not supported here.

| Framework or approach | Useful planning application | Prompt to the author | Limit or safeguard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behaviorist approaches and practice with feedback | Clearly defined procedures or component skills | Which actions need repeated practice and specific feedback? | Do not equate compliance, rewards, or clicks with understanding; retain reasoning and transfer. |
| Cognitivism and schema development | Prior knowledge, concept relationships, and misconceptions | What knowledge structure must learners build before this becomes understandable? | Concept maps and exposition alone do not establish learning. |
| Cognitive load theory | Complex procedures and novice learning | Should we model this first, then fade support? | Match support to prior knowledge; do not maximize difficulty or rely on a simplistic cognitive-load score. |
| Constructivist approaches | Interpreting evidence and revising existing explanations | What initial explanation could students test and revise? | Knowledge construction does not require unguided discovery. Supply needed background and guidance. |
| Social constructivist and social learning approaches | Peer explanation, modeling, critique, and shared reasoning | What would students gain from seeing another person's reasoning? | Provide criteria and correction; group work is not automatically effective or accessible. |
| Experiential and situated learning | Authentic cases, action, reflection, and revised application | What experience or realistic case would make this concept usable? | Activity needs explanation and reflection; simulations require accuracy review. |
| Adult learning and self-directed learning | Relevance, experience, choice, and independent study | Which choices can students make, and what support will they need? | Do not assume adults already know how to plan or monitor learning. |
| Self-regulated learning and metacognition | Planning, checking understanding, adjusting strategies | How can students identify what they cannot yet explain? | Confidence and time on page are not mastery measures. |
| Backward design and constructive alignment | Clarifying purpose before selecting content | What understanding should this unit support, and what content belongs? | A textbook need not own formal assessment of every course outcome. |
| Multimedia learning | Coordinating text, diagrams, narration, and attention | Which representation makes the relationship clearer? | Keep accessibility alternatives; do not remove captions to avoid redundancy. |
| Retrieval, spacing, and interleaving | Recall and distinguishing related concepts over repeated encounters | Which earlier concept should return here, and why? | Use initial blocked practice when helpful; mixing and delay depend on task and expertise. |
| UDL | Choices in engagement, representation, action, and expression | What barrier could prevent a student from using this resource? | UDL is design guidance, not WCAG conformance or learning-style matching. |

Offer no more than two relevant choices at a decision point. Explain them with a brief sample from the author's discipline. Record the selected approach, location, rationale, alternatives considered, source, and author approval. An author may defer a framework choice.

## Evidence and technical references

E1. NSW Department of Education, Cognitive load theory in practice: https://education.nsw.gov.au/about-us/education-data-and-research/cese/publications/practical-guides-for-educators/cognitive-load-theory-in-practice

Its guidance includes tailoring instruction to existing knowledge, worked examples, gradually increasing independence, and reducing inessential information. Use these as conditional planning strategies, not a ban on exploration.

E2. Mayer and Fiorella, Principles for Reducing Extraneous Processing in Multimedia Learning, Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning: https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139547369.015

The publisher summary identifies coherence, signaling, redundancy, and spatial and temporal contiguity. Full chapter access was not established for this package. For detailed claims, retrieve the original work or an authorized full text. Segmenting is also covered in Mayer's Multimedia Learning: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/multimedia-learning/7A62F072A71289E1E262980CB026A3F9/listing

E3. Institute of Education Sciences, Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning: https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/1

The guide recommends spaced learning, combining worked examples and problem solving, graphics with verbal descriptions, abstract/concrete connections, quizzing, and explanatory questions. Its recommendations carry different evidence ratings and derive from particular contexts. Do not claim universal effects or prescribe a fixed spacing schedule for every university course.

E4. Carnegie Mellon University, Learning Principles: https://www.cmu.edu/student-success/other-resources/need-advice/learning-principles.html

Institutional synthesis concerning prior knowledge, knowledge organization, motivation, practice, and monitoring learning. Use as an orientation resource rather than as the original evidence for every theory in the menu.

E5. Carnegie Mellon University, Teaching Principles: https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/principles/teaching.html

Use for discussion of learning objectives, instruction, and assessment alignment. Keep the textbook's support role distinct from course-level assessment responsibility.

A1. W3C, Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2: https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/

Use actual applicable success criteria at levels A and AA. Conformance applies to complete pages and processes, including embedded components; checking generated text alone is insufficient. Fixed caption character counts are production conventions, not a universal WCAG rule.

A2. CAST UDL Guidelines: https://udlguidelines.cast.org/

Use flexible engagement, representation, and action/expression to reduce barriers and support agency. Do not promise that offering formats by itself guarantees learning.
